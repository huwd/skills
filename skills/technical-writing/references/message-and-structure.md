# Message, outline and evidence

Use this when writing a substantial document, or revising one so it serves
its reader. It follows the reader and purpose from
[reader-and-purpose.md](reader-and-purpose.md): that decides who the
document is for; this decides what it says to them and in what order.

## How readers move through a document

Readers rarely start at the top and read every word. They skim the
headings and openings to decide whether the document matters to them,
then read the sections that do, then read closely only where they need
detail. Each pass is a filter:

| Layer | The reader asks | Fails when |
| ----- | --------------- | ---------- |
| Outline: title, headings, openings | Is this for me, and what is it saying? | The headings are generic or follow the order the work happened |
| Points: the claim each section makes | What are the important decisions or findings? | A section has no clear point, or hides it after the detail |
| Detail: facts, figures, sources, examples | How do I know, and how does it work? | Claims are unsupported, or facts sit far from the points they support |

Write and revise from the top layer down. Detail cannot rescue a document
whose message is unclear, and polishing sentences before the structure is
right wastes the effort.

## State the message first

Before outlining, write the document's message in one or two sentences:
what the reader should know or decide once they have read it. For a
record, that is the decision and its main reason; for a report, the
result and how far to trust it.

If you cannot state the message, the document is not ready to write. Find
out what was decided or found, or ask; do not cover the gap with
background.

## Build an outline that develops it

Write the headings before the prose. Read them alone, in order:

- Together they should give a reader the message and how it develops,
  without the paragraphs.
- Name the content, such as "Why reviews do not edit", rather than the
  category, such as "Discussion".
- Order them for the reader's questions, usually the conclusion before
  the method and limits. Never order them by when the work happened.

Open each section with its point, then support it. A reader skimming the
first sentence of each section should get the argument.

## Put evidence where it supports a point

Each fact should support a point the reader needs, or be reference
material they will look up:

- Place a measurement, source or example in the section whose claim it
  supports, not in a separate pile of findings.
- Keep the strength of the evidence visible. State sample sizes, limits
  and open questions as plainly as the results.
- Results always stay: measurements, their conditions and sample sizes,
  even when the source phrases them as narration ("I measured…"). Rewrite
  the narration as a statement of the result.
- Material that only records the author's process, such as the order of
  steps taken or dead ends that taught nothing, can go. Method that affects
  how far to trust a result stays.

## Keep every fact when restructuring

Restructuring is where facts get lost. Before moving anything:

1. List the facts, figures, versions, sources and decisions in the
   original.
2. Restructure.
3. Check every item on the list is still present and unchanged, or was
   deliberately removed for a reason you can state.

Do not invent a decision, reason or result to fill a gap in the outline.
Mark it as an open question instead.

## Keep it proportional

This is for documents that need their structure planned or repaired. For
a correction, an addition or a tidy-up of a document that already works,
change only what the task needs: keep its headings and paragraphs, and add
no orientation headings or summaries it did not ask for.

## Source

The three reading layers and the order of writing (message, then outline,
then detail) adapt Prashanth Rao's
[A framework for technical writing in the age of LLMs](https://thedataquarry.com/blog/a-framework-for-reading-and-writing/)
(25 December 2025). The article describes writing essays and blog posts.
Its ideas are summarised here in original wording and applied to
repository documents; its advice on titles, personal voice and choosing
topics is not adopted. The fact inventory and the proportionality rule are
this skill's own additions.
